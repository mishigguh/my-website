Rethinking Posture: From Static Position to Dynamic Organization

By Misha Lantsov · Movement teacher and bodyworker in Colorado

I hear about posture constantly from bodywork clients: “I have bad posture” or “I’m trying to maintain good posture.” These conversations often lead to the same place, so I want to share a different way of thinking about this common concern—one that I find more useful in practice.

Beyond good and bad posture

Posture is not simply a static position you hold. There is no single position that every person should maintain all day. A more useful frame is organization: how your body arranges itself to meet the demands of a particular moment.

That organization is influenced by several things at once:

  • External constraints such as chairs, desks, floors, loads, and tasks.
  • Current capacity such as strength, mobility, endurance, and available movement options.
  • Habit and perception: the strategies your nervous system has learned and the options you notice or do not notice.

Strategies over standards

Think of the body as having a toolkit of strategies for any given situation. Sitting, standing, carrying, reaching, and running all require the body to distribute load and solve a problem. The question is less “Is this position correct?” and more “Is this strategy working, and do I have other options when the demand changes?”

Understanding consequences

A position is not automatically harmful because it looks rounded, asymmetrical, or different from an idealized model. Problems are more likely to matter when a person has few options, a strategy is uncomfortable or fatiguing, or the demands placed on the body consistently exceed its current capacity.

Practical application

Rather than trying to hold yourself in one “good” position, experiment. Shift your weight. Change the height of the screen or chair. Breathe in a different shape. Stand up. Reach. Walk. Notice which positions feel easy, which feel effortful, and whether you can move between them without needing to force yourself into alignment.

In practice
